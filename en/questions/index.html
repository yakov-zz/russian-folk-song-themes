Questions we asked the songs

Each question below was stated before it was tested. Five were registered in a written plan before any test, and 2 of them held up; the others are the author’s own hypotheses, stated before testing but defined in detail only when they were tested. The statistical details are in the paper; here each answer is in plain words.

“Held up” means the result cleared the bar set in advance: a clear difference, adjusted for asking several questions at once, that does not hang on any single book.

Registered before the tests

Do Cossack-host songs sing more about soldiering and war?

Held up

Yes. Recruitment, campaigns and frontier service are the primary theme of 24.5% of Cossack-host songs and 4.7% of the others, a gap of about 17.6 points after allowing for genre. It holds when any one collection is left out.

How the two sides were defined
Main songs of all three languages, reprints merged. Cossack-host songs are those the editors attribute to a Cossack community (Don, Kuban, Terek, Orenburg, Ural and others); every other song is on the other side.
Songs and books on each side
  • Cossack hosts: 1,321 songs, 16 books
  • Other communities: 10,050 songs, 25 books
What this means
Soldiering is one of the defining subjects of Cossack song in these books. The gap is large in raw shares and stays large when ritual, game and other songs are compared separately.
Example songs (soldiering songs)
  • Cossack hosts: Ты расти-ка, рябина раскудрявая · Охъ ты, служба наша нужная, сторона… · Ой ты, калинушка, размалинушка
  • Other communities: Какъ не самъ-то я по дѣвицу… · Одъ Киева до Питера мостили мости · — Ой, прощай Омский и Тобольский
Registered before the tests

Are Cossack songs more often sung in a man’s voice?

Held up

Yes. Of the songs with a gendered voice, 44.1% of Cossack-host songs speak in a man’s voice, against 22.1% of the others: about 17.1 points more after allowing for genre.

How the two sides were defined
Same two sides as the question above, counting only songs whose voice is clearly a woman’s or a man’s. Choral songs with no single voice and songs where the voice is unclear are left out.
Songs and books on each side
  • Cossack hosts: 589 songs, 15 books
  • Other communities: 6,082 songs, 24 books
What this means
Most Russian folk songs in these books speak in a woman’s voice; Cossack songs are the main exception, which fits their subjects: service, campaigns and comrades.
Example songs (songs in a man’s voice)
  • Cossack hosts: Чи це ж тая удивонъка · Какъ я понѣче, добрый молодецъ, всею… · «Селезень мой, селезень
  • Other communities: Я поѣду во Китай городъ гуляти · Ай ну, ай ну, на клену · Встану, я добрый молодецъ
Registered before the tests

Are bridal laments more common in the north?

Not clear

No. 12.6% in the north and north-west against 11.2% in central and southern Russia.

How the two sides were defined
Main songs from the North and North-west against songs from the Centre and South, by the region the editors give for each song. Volga, Urals and Siberia are on neither side.
Songs and books on each side
  • North and north-west: 1,774 songs, 18 books
  • Centre and south: 1,540 songs, 15 books
What this means
Bridal laments are not more common in the northern books than in the central and southern ones. The northern lament tradition may still be richer in form, but it is not larger in share here.
Example songs (bridal laments)
  • North and north-west: Растопися, парна баенка · О й У Ж Г Л… · Не куна жалобилася
  • Centre and south: Что на синемъ-то на морѣ · Родимые вы мои, брателки · Какъ шелъ казакъ дорогою

Did Siberian songs sing more of prison and exile?

Not clear

Not clearly. 6.2% against 1.3% in European Russia, but across collections the gap could be zero, and it leans on one Siberian book.

How the two sides were defined
Russian-language main songs from Siberia and the Far East against non-Cossack songs from European Russia (North, North-west, Centre, South, Volga).
Songs and books on each side
  • Siberia: 486 songs, 11 books
  • European Russia: 3,917 songs, 19 books
What this means
Siberian books print more prison and exile songs, but much of the gap comes from one Siberian collection, so the books cannot show that this was a Siberian trait rather than one collector’s interest.
Example songs (prison and exile songs)
  • Siberia: Идет он усталый, и цепи звенят · Не бѣлая лебедка въ перелетъ летитъ · Соловей ты мой, соловейко
  • European Russia: Какъ у насъ то было на… · Сидѣлъ воронъ на березѣ · Гуленьки, э-ой, мои голубо(й)... голубоц(и)ки
Registered before the tests

Do Soviet-era recordings hold more love songs than pre-1918 ones?

Not clear

Not clearly. 23.3% against 17.5%, with only a few collections on each side.

How the two sides were defined
Main songs with a recording year (or a recording interval, taken at its middle): recorded between 1946 and 1991 against recorded before 1918. Songs recorded 1918–1945 or with no date are left out.
Songs and books on each side
  • Recorded 1946–1991: 643 songs, 9 books
  • Recorded before 1918: 624 songs, 16 books
What this means
Later recordings do hold more love songs, but each side rests on few collections and the gap changes a lot when one is left out, so this is not a clear finding.
Example songs (love songs)
  • Recorded 1946–1991: «Да какой ты мой друг · Из-за леса, леса темного · ОХ, ЭКА МИЛЕНЬКАЯ О й ДА…
  • Recorded before 1918: Аннушка дочь княжелецкая и добрый молодец · Ай да напади-тко ты, роса · Сказали про молодца, будто без вести…
Author’s hypothesis, stated before testing

Do women sing more about cheating than men?

Not clear

Mostly because most songs are in a woman’s voice. 44.5% of all songs speak in a woman’s voice, and so do 64.6% of the 811 songs about unfaithfulness and jealousy; men’s voices are 14.1% of all songs and 15.9% of the cheating songs. Counting only songs with a woman’s or a man’s voice, women voice 76% of all songs and 80% of the cheating ones. Within each voice, 10.3% of women’s songs and 8.0% of men’s songs are about cheating: a small extra lean towards women that is not clear. Where the actual singer is named, the gap is larger (4.4% of women’s songs against 1.3% of men’s) but falls just short of clear when tested; only about 700 songs name a single singer, and most of the men are Orenburg Cossacks from one collection (Myakutin).

How the two sides were defined
The voice of the song (a woman’s or a man’s) is read by the same three models that read the theme. The singer is read from the performer’s name where one person is named; only about 700 songs name a single singer.
Songs and books on each side
  • Woman’s voice: 5,064 songs, 28 books
  • Man’s voice: 1,607 songs, 28 books
  • Woman singer: 477 songs, 11 books
  • Man singer: 235 songs, 7 books
What this means
Cheating songs are mostly women’s songs because most songs are women’s songs. Measured against that base rate the lean towards women is small; it is larger for named singers, but still not clear, and that comparison is close to a comparison of two kinds of book.
Example songs (cheating songs)
  • Woman’s voice: Батько милий, батько милий, а мати… · У дѣвушки у голубушки · Не-сиес
  • Man’s voice: Ужъ я думалъ думу-думушку · Летівъ орелъ по-надъ море, та й… · «Селезень мой, селезень
Main songs with a theme, all three languages (11,371); cheating songs = primary theme “unfaithfulness, jealousy and betrayal” (811).

Author’s hypothesis, stated before testing

Were religious songs absent from Soviet editions?

Description

They were, but not only there. Religious verse is near zero in every era of this corpus: 0.3% of songs in pre-1918 editions and 0.3% in Soviet ones. Nineteenth-century editors of secular song also left spiritual verse out. Only some post-1991 editions print it (4.8%).

How the two sides were defined
Edition era is fixed by the book’s publication year: before 1918, Soviet (1918–1991) and after 1991. A song counts if religious verse is any of its themes, not only the main one.
Songs and books on each side
  • Editions before 1918: 9,365 songs, 15 books
  • Soviet editions: 634 songs, 11 books
  • Editions after 1991: 1,372 songs, 6 books
What this means
Soviet editions did not print religious verse, but neither did most earlier editors of secular song. Only some post-1991 books print it, so its absence is an editorial habit older than Soviet censorship.
Example songs (religious verses)
  • Editions before 1918: Вдова молодая, жена богомольная · А ще сонце не заходило, а… · женихy и невѣстѣ
  • Soviet editions: Ой Ти,Боже,милосердний
Author’s hypothesis, stated before testing

Did editors in different eras select different songs?

Not clear

No clear trace. Bawdy songs are absent from Soviet editions (0%) but rare before 1918 too (0.4%), and the gap falls just short of clear when tested. Soviet editions print more historical songs (5.0% against 2.2%), fewer satires (0.9% against 3.7%) and fewer songs of hardship (16.4% against 23.2%), but none of these is clear, because era is fixed by the book and each era is a handful of books.

How the two sides were defined
The same three edition eras, compared for the bawdy register and for three themes chosen beforehand: historical songs, satires and songs of hardship.
Songs and books on each side
  • Editions before 1918: 9,365 songs, 15 books
  • Soviet editions: 634 songs, 11 books
What this means
No difference is clear. Soviet editions print no bawdy songs at all, but such songs are under 1% of earlier editions too, so even that gap falls just short of the test. The other gaps show in the raw shares, but each era is a handful of books, and the books differ in region and aim as much as in date.
Example songs (bawdy songs)
  • Editions before 1918: Піду я до млина · Голова ль, моя головушка · Ты дуракъ, дружко
Author’s hypothesis, stated before testing

Do non-Cossack songs near the Cossack hosts use more Cossack words?

Not clear

They seem to: 11.7% of non-Cossack songs from regions near the hosts mention Cossacks, atamans or the like, against 1.8% far from them. But the gap varies too much between collections to count as clear.

How the two sides were defined
Non-Cossack songs only. Near: South, Volga, Urals, the Dnieper banks and Sloboda Ukraine; far: North, North-west and Centre. A song counts if its text uses Cossack words (казак/козак, атаман, есаул, хорунжий, запорожец, січовик).
Songs and books on each side
  • Regions near the hosts: 3,427 songs, 13 books
  • Regions far from them: 2,392 songs, 20 books
What this means
Cossacks are a familiar figure in songs sung near the hosts, but how familiar depends strongly on the collection, so the pattern is suggestive, not established.
Example songs (songs with Cossack words)
  • Regions near the hosts: Батько милий, батько милий, а мати… · Отъѣзжаетъ казаченька на разгуляньице · неженатому гостю или боярину
  • Regions far from them: Ахъ ты, хмѣль, мой хмѣль, веселая… · Ходилъ, гулялъ по Дону · (напевы 31)

Is the Ukrainian «козак» a soldier?

Description

Mostly not. In Ukrainian lyric «козак» often simply means the young man, the lover. 25.2% of Chubinsky’s Ukrainian songs use Cossack words, yet 57% of those songs are love songs, and only 6.3% of his songs are about soldiering. Among Russian Cossack-host songs, 39.4% use Cossack words and only 9% of those are love songs.

How the two sides were defined
Songs whose text uses Cossack words. Ukrainian side: Chubinsky’s non-Cossack songs. Russian side: Cossack-host songs in Russian. The share counts the songs whose main theme is love.
Songs and books on each side
  • Ukrainian (Chubinsky): 612 songs, one book only
  • Russian Cossack hosts: 415 songs, 13 books
What this means
In Ukrainian lyric the козак is usually the beloved, not a soldier. The Ukrainian side is one collection, so this is a description, not a test.
Example songs (Cossack words in a love song)
  • Ukrainian (Chubinsky): Батько милий, батько милий, а мати… · 1 Пудъ гаємъ зелененькимъ · Тихо, тихо Дунай воду несе
  • Russian Cossack hosts: «Да какой ты мой друг · Полянка, полянка, дѣвчонка моя · Сталися барвінку нызэнько

How do European Russia and Siberia differ?

Not clear

Less than expected. Ballads, religious verse and tall tales are about as common in both (6.1% in European Russia, 4.9% in Siberia). Historical songs are rare everywhere, and absent from the Siberian books (0.0% against 0.5%). Byliny, the epic songs, appear only in the European Russian books (72 records against 0).

How the two sides were defined
Russian-language, non-Cossack main songs. European Russia: North, North-west, Centre, South and Volga; Siberia: Siberia and the Far East. Byliny were not given themes, so they are counted among all records instead.
Songs and books on each side
  • European Russia: 3,917 songs, 19 books
  • Siberia: 452 songs, 9 books
What this means
The two look alike in these books. The one sharp difference, byliny, reflects what each collector set out to record: the Siberian books here are collections of lyric songs.
Example songs (ballads, verses and tall tales)
  • European Russia: Ой, комаръ-комарище · У“ мо · Удалъ, удалѣлъ да и Пётро князь
  • Siberia: Дударь мой, дударь молодой · Я живалъ-бывалъ въ Казани, выросъ въ… · За рѣкою, за рѣченькою

How many songs are about the supernatural?

Description

About 2.9% of the main songs (between 1.7% and 5.0%), and almost no chastushki. Most are love magic, a girl or wife turned into a tree or a bird, and sorcery; spirits and demons are rare. This was estimated from a sample, not from every song.

How the two sides were defined
Every song with a supernatural cue word (sorcery, devil, serpent, spirit, transformation, the dead) was read again by the three models, together with a random sample of 500 other records. The share found in the random sample is applied to all songs without a cue word.
Songs and books on each side
  • Songs with a supernatural cue word: 371 songs
  • Random songs without one: 358 songs
What this means
Supernatural songs are a small and mostly hidden group: most of them do not use the obvious words, which is why a random sample was needed. The estimate is uncertain, from under 2% to about 5%.
Example songs (supernatural songs the cue words miss)
  • Random songs without one: Упала стріла зъ синёго неба · Исходилъ я, молодецъ, день до вечера · Калину съ малиною вода поняла · Изъ далеча, далеча изъ чиста поля

The books are not a random sample of what people sang: a pattern that holds describes these collections.
