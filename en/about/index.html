About & method

Who collected these songs

The songs come from printed editions, not from field recordings made for this project. The oldest volumes are the work of nineteenth-century collectors and editors: Sobolevsky’s seven volumes of Great Russian songs (1895–1902), which gathered texts from many earlier collections and manuscripts; the songs collected by Kireevsky, published long after his death; Shein’s volumes; and Chubinsky’s expedition to the south-western provinces of the Empire.

Soviet-era editions come from folklore expeditions and archives, such as the phonogram recordings from the Pinega, and from regional collections of Cossack and village song. They were selected under their own rules: some themes were favoured, and obscene or religious material was usually left out.

After 1991, editors published what had been kept out, such as Toporkov’s anthology of erotic folklore (1995), alongside new regional collections from Siberia, the Urals and the Kuban.

Each edition reflects what its collector looked for and what its censor and publisher allowed. This site measures what these editors chose to print. That is not the same as what people sang.

How the songs were read

Pages were digitised from the editions and split into songs. Reprints of the same text in several books are merged for the statistics.

Three AI models from three different companies each read every song and gave it a primary theme, any secondary themes and several flags, following a fixed codebook of 20 themes for songs and 9 for chastushki: Google Gemini 3.8 Flash (gemini-3.8-flash-low), Anthropic Claude Haiku (the haiku model of Claude Code; the exact release behind that name was not logged) and OpenAI gpt-6-luna (through the Codex command-line tool). All three coded the corpus in November 2026.

A theme stands when at least two of the three models gave it. Where all three disagreed (1,056 songs), a fourth model that had coded nothing, Anthropic Claude Sonnet 5.5 (claude-sonnet-5-5), chose blindly among their answers.

Main songs and chastushki are always counted apart, and the two chastushka anthologies are never added together.

Five questions were registered in a written plan before any test was run. The author’s own hypotheses were also stated before testing, but their exact definitions (which songs, eras and themes) were fixed only when they were tested, so they are reported as a separate family and not called confirmatory.

Limitations and biases

  • Editors’ choices. The corpus is what editors printed. Nineteenth-century editors under-printed obscene and anticlerical songs; Soviet editions print no bawdy songs at all, and several are devoted to one ritual or one district; anthologies such as Bakhtin’s and Toporkov’s are thematic by design.
  • Sources and strata overlap. Several groups are one book each: the Don songs are Listopadov’s, the Orenburg songs Myakutin’s, the Belarusian songs one volume. Comparisons between such groups are comparisons between books.
  • Some books are built around one theme. The overall shares partly reflect which books exist, not what people sang. Three books (Shein’s wedding volume of 1900, Kireevsky’s songs published in 1911 and the Siberian Belarusian volume of 2011) hold 81% of all the wedding songs, and in each of them wedding songs are at least 67% of the book. That is one reason love and wedding lead the overall figures. The balanced shares, in which every book counts equally, and the table by book show how much this matters. We plan to add collections from more regions and of more kinds.
  • Places and dates. 45% of the songs have no place printed, and most have no recording date; edition era is the date of the book.
  • Machine reading. The texts were digitised by OCR and may contain recognition errors, especially in pre-reform spelling. The themes were assigned by AI models, not by folklorists; agreement between the models is reported in the paper, and short or fragmentary songs are the hardest to classify.
  • Language is not nationality. Songs are grouped by the language of the text. The books rarely record how singers identified themselves.
  • Known gaps in this version. 35 songs end mid-text and no continuation was found in the book. 1,119 song boundaries sit next to a page the text recognition missed and were not re-read, so a song there may be cut short or joined to its neighbour. 64 songs from the Siberian Belarusian volume have not yet been checked against the page scans. Corrections are welcome through the repository’s Issues.

Related work

Folklorists have long sorted Russian lyric songs by theme: Sobolevsky arranged his seven volumes by subject, and Kolpakova’s classification and index of Russian songs drew on about 30,000 texts. These works describe and catalogue songs, but they do not count themes. As far as we found, no earlier study has coded Russian folk songs from many printed collections song by song against a fixed list of themes and reported theme shares with their uncertainty. Computational studies of song themes exist for other traditions; they used topic models, which find word clusters without a fixed list of themes and give no per-song theme that can be checked. The closest studies:

  • Petrovych (2025): a topic model of 2,762 Ukrainian songs from Podillia; data and code on Zenodo.
  • Dvořáková et al. (2026): about 28,500 folk song texts in Czech, Dutch, Estonian, Korean and Ukrainian, machine-translated and compared by topic.
  • Lassche (2022): topics of 43,772 Dutch printed songs, 1550–1750.
  • Strle and Marolt: themes of Slovenian ballad variants.
  • Kolpakova (1983): a genre and plot index of Russian songs.
  • The full comparison, with what we may borrow from these studies, is in RELATED_WORK.md.

Glossary

Chastushka
A short rhymed song, usually of four lines, often humorous or topical, sung to a few stock tunes. It spread in villages and factory towns from the late nineteenth century. Chastushki are counted apart from the other songs, and the two chastushka anthologies are never added together.
Bylina
An epic song about heroes such as Ilya Muromets, recorded mostly in the Russian North. Byliny are in the corpus but were not given themes.
Lament
A wailed song of grief or parting: a bride leaving her family, a death, a recruit taken into the army. Bridal laments are part of the wedding ritual.
Khorovod
A round dance with singing, and the songs sung for it.
Guberniya, uezd
A province of the Russian Empire (until the late 1920s) and a district within it. Places on the map are the administrative centres of guberniyas or, for later books, oblasts.
Oblast
A province of the Soviet Union and of Russia today.
Cossack host
A self-governing military community of Cossacks with its own land: the Don, Kuban, Terek, Orenburg and Ural hosts among others. “Cossack-host songs” are songs the editors attribute to such a community.
Primary theme
The one theme that best describes a song, given by at least two of the three models. A song may also carry secondary themes; shares on this site count primary themes unless they say otherwise.
Reprint
The same song printed in more than one book. Reprints are merged and counted once.
Pre-reform spelling
Russian spelling before 1918, with the letters ѣ, і and ѳ and a final ъ. Older books are printed in it, and the texts here keep it.
Edition era
When the book was published: before 1918, Soviet (1918–1991) or after 1991. It is not the date the song was recorded.
