Uma canção, muitas versões

Uma canção popular nunca foi um texto fixo. Muitos livros imprimem várias versões de uma canção, e a mesma canção aparece em livros de regiões diferentes. O corpus contém 1 674 canções impressas em mais de uma versão no mesmo livro e 4 091 ligações entre versões de livros diferentes. Aqui são comparadas verso a verso.

Esta página descreve; não testa nada. As versões foram encontradas automaticamente e verificadas por leitores em amostras. Os editores escolheram o que imprimir, a ortografia antiga e dialetal pode parecer uma alteração, e a região mistura-se com o livro, o recolhedor e a década.

Um verso conta como encontrado se a outra versão tiver um verso que partilhe pelo menos metade dos seus radicais; os leitores confirmaram 56 de 59 destas correspondências. A queda mantém-se nos pares encontrados apenas pelo texto inteiro (67,5% para 40,9%) e nas versões de extensão semelhante (46,4% para 24,5%), pelo que não se deve apenas ao facto de alguns textos terem sido impressos truncados. As versões que os próprios editores assinalaram não a mostram: os editores imprimem muitas vezes uma variante apenas com os versos que diferem.

O que muda

Sobretudo a mesma palavra noutra forma

Quando um verso sobrevive com uma palavra alterada e todas as outras no mesmo lugar, que palavra é essa? Entre livros, 45,3% das trocas são a mesma palavra noutra forma ou uma palavra com o mesmo sentido (девица → девушка, сорвал → оборвал). 14,0% são duas palavras que desempenham funções diferentes no verso, como um adjetivo numa versão e um substantivo na outra (быстрая → реченька). Nomes, lugares, papéis sociais e parentescos perfazem 9,4%. Os nomes de lugares são raros, e não mais comuns entre regiões (1,4%) do que dentro de uma (1,3%): raramente se tornou local uma canção mudando o nome de um rio ou de uma cidade.

Os nomes são o padrão mais nítido: nas canções de casamento, o nome da própria noiva (Настасьюшка, Прасковьюшка) está onde outra versão canta лебедушка, o cisne.

Quem canta

Cerca de uma versão em vinte muda o cantor

Extensão

Mais longa em alguns livros, não em algumas regiões

Quando duas versões de uma canção diferem em extensão, qual é a mais longa? Depende sobretudo do livro. De início, o Norte e a Sibéria pareciam mais longos, mas 88% das versões siberianas vêm de um só livro, Canções líricas russas da Sibéria (1997), e os livros publicados depois de 1991 têm a versão mais longa em dois pares de três. A dianteira do Norte é menor, mas mantém-se quando ambas as versões vêm de livros do mesmo período; 22% das versões do norte vêm de Istomin e Lyapunov (1899).

As três maiores coletâneas: quantas canções cada uma tem só para si e quantas partilha (círculos fora de escala)

Partilhada significa que pelo menos uma versão está ligada a uma versão do outro livro; as ligações estão certas em cerca de 9 em cada 10 casos. Os sete volumes de Sobolevsky são tomados em conjunto nos círculos. Nos círculos, uma canção é um grupo de versões ligadas apenas dentro destas três coletâneas, pelo que outro livro não pode juntar duas canções.

Lado a lado

Vinte e duas canções em várias versões

22 canções encontradas em pelo menos três livros. Para cada uma, um modelo de IA (Claude) escreveu o que fica e o que muda, um segundo modelo tentou encontrar erros nisso a partir dos textos, e o resultado foi lido antes de ser publicado. Os versos realçados encontram-se também noutra versão mostrada.

Don't trample my mint, curly-head Не ходи, бѣлъ-кудреватый,3 versões, 3 livros

In every version a girl tells a fair, curly-haired young man not to walk past her place and not to trample what she has planted with her own hands and watered. In two versions she tells him outright that she did not plant or water it for him. In the third she says it was for no one but her dear friend. What changes is the place he must keep away from (her garden or her house), what grows there (fragrant mint or simply her seedlings), and whom she planted it for: the one she loved and kissed, a dear friend, or a dear friend and her own father. One version stops there. The two longer ones go on. One goes on to a guest who seldom visits, stays a single night and spends it in longing, then opens the window and waits for dawn. The other goes on to a porch and an entry hall full of guests who are "злые сопостаты" (wicked enemies) rather than matchmakers or brothers.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The opening plea: "Не ходи, бѣлъ-кудреватый / бѣлъ-кудрявый"
  • The ban on trampling: "Не топчи" (the mint or the seedlings)
  • The girl's own planting and watering: "садила ... поливала"
  • She names whom she planted it for: "Я не для тебя садила" / "для дружка милаго"

The widow and her roofless house По лугу, лужочку, по крутому бережочку,9 versões, 3 livros

In every version a young widow speaks to her house (хоромы). She asks whether it has no master, and in most versions she also asks why it stands unroofed or open. In some versions she adds that her children have no father and she has no dear friend. In six versions a man then appears and offers to be master of the house. He may be a young bachelor, two young merchants or a bold young lad, and in some versions he also offers to be father to the children. The endings differ. Some widows refuse him with a berry image: guelder-rose (калина) cannot match raspberry, or grass cannot match guelder-rose. One says her dead husband cannot be replaced. Some versions end on the man's promise or question, with no reply. In Shein's text no man appears, and the widow ends by saying a stranger cannot replace the children's own father. In two versions a reply comes instead, saying the master is away in a far land or has died beyond the river Laba.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The widow addresses the house: "хоромы мои, хоромушки"
  • The question about the missing master: "хозяина нѣту"
  • In most versions the house stands unroofed or open: "не покрытые стоите" / "раскрыты стоите"
  • She is "вдова молодая" (a young widow), and in most versions she walks and grieves ("ходила-гуляла")

The dying Cossack sends his horse home За Ураломъ, за рѣкой,14 versões, 4 livros

In most versions a young man by a river tears up grass for a fire, tends his wounds and speaks to them, often saying they have reached his heart. Then, as he dies, he orders his horse to run home to his family. The river changes from text to text (the Ural, the Kuban, the Danube, or just "a river"), and so does the hero: a Cossack, an uhlan or a hussar. His companions change too: a horse and a sabre in some texts, a girl called Sashenka in others. The endings differ most. In many versions the wife hears that her husband has "married another", with the bullet, the sabre or the grave as his bride. In one the horse simply says its master lies in the damp earth under a coffin lid. In others he leaves the wife free to remarry or stay a widow, and one version ends with his death and has no horse or message at all.

O que as versões mantêm

  • Tearing grass for the fire: "травоньку рвалъ" / "травушку рвал"
  • Speaking to the wounds: "Ужъ вы, раны мои"
  • Addressing the horse: "Ужъ ты, конь ли, мой конь"
  • The horse sent home to the parents and the young wife

The water fetched too late Бѣлъ горючъ ками разгорается;13 versões, 5 livros

A woman sings about a man who falls ill and asks for water, or who sends her out for it. She goes to fetch it, usually counting the hours: in the first hour she sets out, in the second she draws the water, in the third she comes back. By the time she returns he is dead or dying. In most versions the man is her "dear friend" (милый друг). In two he is a hated husband (постылый муж), in one also an old one, and she decides not to mourn him. The openings differ. Some begin with a burning stone under an apple, pear or birch tree. Others begin with a pine forest burning on a hill, with a girl robbing nightingale nests, or with the bed the husband built. Many versions end with her stopping her tears and saying she can find a new lover or husband, and several add that she cannot get a new father and mother. Other versions end at the deathbed, with the dying man's orders to be buried in the open field, or with a different song added on.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The water errand counted in hours: "Во первомъ часу за водой пошла"
  • Drawing the water in the second hour: "воду черпала"
  • She comes back to find the man dead or laid out: "переставился" / "наряжонъ лежитъ"
  • The singer is a young woman speaking about her man in the first person

The widow who did not know her husband and son Садилось солнце ясное9 versões, 6 livros

In most versions two soldiers or 'heroes' (three 'eroli' in one text) knock at a lonely widow's hut and ask to stay the night. She says the hut is cold and she has nothing cooked, and they say they need nothing. In the fuller texts she tells them that her husband and son went off to serve, and the two guests turn out to be those very men. The visitors change from 'grenadiers' to 'heroes' to 'eroli', and the years of absence vary: seventeen, twenty-five or thirty. The endings differ most. Some versions stop at 'we are here before you'. In one, she finds a gold ring tied up in the soldier's hat and runs to wake the children. In another she says she did not recognise them: the husband had gone so grey, and the son had become such a hero. Some versions open with a sunset and the birds falling silent, others go straight to the hut under a tree. Two texts dwell on the widow waiting for her dear one and on the soldiers approaching. In one of these the soldiers are sent on to the village elder for the night.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The visiting soldiers ask the hostess for a night's lodging: «пусти ... ночевать»
  • She answers that she has not heated the hut: «хату не топила» / «печку не топила»
  • They address her as hostess: «любезная наша хозяюшка» / «хозяйка»
  • She grieves for her husband and son taken into service: «мужа ... сына своего»

The kuma's sick sparrow Здорово, кума.4 versões, 3 livros

Every version is a dialogue with a kuma, a woman friend who is godparent to one's child. She is asked how her sparrow is, or how she is herself, and what aches. In the longer versions the question is repeated stanza by stanza, each time with a new aching part. The Volga text has head, heart, heels and wings. The Vyatka text has heels and belly. The Vologda text has head, hands, feet, back and neck. The cure changes from text to text. In two versions the sick one is told to pick a herb in the kitchen garden and steam the sore part. In the Vologda text the aching part simply "wants" a gift, such as a kerchief or shoes. The endings differ too: two versions close with a lament that the sparrow will never fly or peck peas again, one with "God does not give health", and the 1954 Volga text with a sweetheart who comes and brings health back.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The kuma is addressed by name: "кума" / "кумушка"
  • The sparrow is the sick one or the song's refrain: "воробей"
  • A question about what is wrong: "Что у него болит?" / "Цево у тебя болитъ?" / "Что жъ онъ дѣлаетъ?"
  • The pain is named with repeated "болит", e.g. "Головушка болитъ" or "Болятъ, болятъ пяточки"

The prisoner's letter home and the sweetheart's ransom Ты воспой, воспой, младъ жавороночекъ,8 versões, 3 livros

Most versions open with a bird asked to sing on a patch of thawed ground and send its voice to a young man held in prison. Every one counts his years there with the same "not one year, not two" formula. In all but the shortest text he writes to his father and mother, who disown him because their family has never had a thief or a robber. In most of them he then writes to a sweetheart or to his young wife. In the fullest texts the sweetheart frees him, either by having her chests of treasure opened or by selling her house and garden. In others the wife only weeps over his letter, or the song stops before any answer comes. The bird changes (a lark in most texts, a nightingale in two), and so do the length of the sentence (three, nine or thirty years), who is asked to pay the ransom, and whether the prisoner speaks for himself or is described by someone else. The texts range from a 9-line fragment that only gives a girl as the cause of his imprisonment to a 44-line version that adds a lament and a vow to become a monk.

O que as versões mantêm

  • in most texts the bird is asked to sing on a thawed patch: «на проталинке»
  • in several texts he asks to be comforted: «утешь меня, молодца» / «взвесели меня, молодца»

  • he is held in prison or locked up: «во неволюшке» / «в крепкой крепости» / «за решеточкой»

Heat up, little bathhouse: the bride weeps before her parents Растопись, банюшка,14 versões, 3 livros

Most versions begin by calling on the bathhouse to heat up, or by saying it has been heated. One text starts straight with the scattered pearls, and another first has the singer heating the bath herself. Then a bride weeps before her father or mother about her coming marriage. In many versions she asks why beer is being brewed and spirits distilled. The parent usually answers that she is to be married and names the groom. The names of the bride and groom change from text to text, and so does the groom's rank: a lord, a merchant's son, or a peasant. Some versions drop the question and answer. In those, the bride pleads not to be given away "to strangers", or she blames her father for drinking her away. One text adds a refrain after every line.

  • She weeps before a parent: "Передъ своимъ родныммъ батюшкой" / "Передъ матушкой стоючи"

The sweet words that burned a girl's heart without fire Вы, молодчики, вы, молоденькіе, вы, дружки мои,11 versões, 3 livros

A young woman sings that the kind words of young men have set her heart alight without fire and blown her thoughts across the open fields. Most versions go on to ask who could ease her grief and turn her sweetheart back from the road, then picture her lying at the window, gazing at the far fields, and cursing the distant foreign land that parted her from someone. Who she is parted from changes: in some versions it is her sweetheart, in others her father and mother, and in one also a young husband. Some versions start with a lament for carefree girlhood and a marriage her parents have arranged. Others end with new images, such as a dream in which deep rivers are her tears, or a sweetheart who does not grieve for her. The texts run from 12 to almost 40 lines.

O que as versões mantêm

  • The singer's heart is hurt or burned: "сердце изожгли" / "сердечушко"
  • Thoughts or grief spread over the open fields: "по чистымъ полямъ"
  • In most versions, being parted from loved ones: "Разлучила" / "разлучить"
  • A female singer speaking in the first person

The girls sowed flax: bast shoes for one's own wife Посѣяли дѣвки ленъ13 versões, 6 livros

The versions start with girls sowing flax. A young man comes into the field and picks or tramples the flowers. In almost all versions he throws them into a river, which usually will not take them and washes them ashore. In most versions a horse then strikes a stone that gives no spark, and the song turns to a married couple: there is "no truth" in one's own wife, so she gets bast shoes while another man's wife gets fine shoes. The intruder is called a lad, a dandy, a young Cossack or Ivanushka. The river is usually the Danube but once the Oka, and the yard belongs to a gentleman, a major or a boyar. In some texts a wife complains about her husband, not a husband about his wife. Some versions repeat their lines with a refrain, each with its own words, and the versions differ a lot in length. The endings vary widely: a man telling his wife to keep the shoes for feast days, a wife dancing the shoes to pieces, a couple drinking the shoes away, a wife getting even with a sackcloth shirt for her husband, or simply a kiss.

O que as versões mantêm

  • Opening line about girls sowing flax, in all but one text: "Посеяли девки лен"

In every version a daughter leaves her mother's home and stops on the way, usually in a dark forest. There she talks to nightingales and asks one to fly to her home side and carry a bow to her parents. Most versions open with a storm cloud rising from behind the forest, but some leave out the storm and begin with her leaving. The songs differ most at the end. A young woman is given in marriage into a large household: father-in-law, mother-in-law, several brothers-in-law, sisters-in-law and aunts. Every version begins with mists, often mists that cannot rise from the blue sea, as an image of grief that will not leave the heart. The place is named as the Asiatic side, the Bukharan side or the Saratov steppe, and the endings differ: greetings sent to the parents, a bird told to fly home with word that he has "married" the far steppe, or a plea to a nightingale not to sing.

The bride who storms the city to bring gifts to her in-laws Подойду, подойду подъ бѣлъ городъ каменный8 versões, 3 livros

In these songs a young woman says she will go up to a city, a stone city or Tsar-gorod, knock out its wall and bring out goods. Most often she takes them from her own father, mother, brother or sister and gives them to her husband's relatives. The gifts and the people who get them change: a horse, a cow, a fox-fur coat, a barrel, a saddle, a spinning wheel or a golden wreath, for the father-in-law alone or for each in-law in turn. What she hopes for also changes. In some versions the in-laws praise God for such a clever daughter-in-law. In others she only hopes they will be kind to her, and in one she also asks them never to scold her. One version reverses the gift-giving: she gives the goods to her own father, mother, brother and sister and teases her in-laws instead. The versions run from a single round of eleven lines to four full rounds of forty lines, and the wall is broken with a spear in some texts and a boot in others.

The mother-in-law's pie and the son-in-law's revenge Теща про зятя пирогъ пекла9 versões, 3 livros

In every version the mother-in-law makes a costly pie for her son-in-law. She tallies its cost line by line in roubles, and in most versions she expects it to feed five or seven people, but he eats it all at one sitting. She asks why he did not burst. In most versions he answers by inviting her for Maslenitsa (Shrovetide; the Great Lent in one text) to be "treated" with clubs and a whip. The treat varies: four birch clubs and a whip in most texts, but elm, birch and aspen clubs in one, oak clubs in another, and only a two-handed whip in a third. Some versions open with girls sowing hops and singing that young men are not marrying, and two of them name the bride (Dunyasha, Parasha). Others start straight with the pie. Several versions go on to show the mother-in-law beaten, fleeing home and pleading a hangover when the son-in-law calls her back. In the Siberian text most lines start with "Эх", and it ends with a second story about a son-in-law complaining that his wife beat him.

The husband who beat his wife for an hour and wept for a week Как на горе, горе8 versões, 3 livros

Nearly every version opens with a stream running under or beside a birch, an oak or a fir. One version, from Kazan, starts with the river alone. Every version says that the water may not be drunk and, beside it, that a wife may not be beaten. A husband then sings that he beat his wife for a short time and wept for much longer, wearing out his eyes or his heart, or spending everything on medicines. The time spans change (once, an hour, a day, a little while; a week, four weeks, a year), and so do the opening tree and hill. After this shared core the endings go far apart. A long text from Siberia has the husband give in and bow to his wife. One from Perm has him hand her power over him. One from Kazan ends with him going off to kiss a girl. A long text from Kursk drifts into a joking song about a lazy bride who will not reap.

O que as versões mantêm

  • a stream under a tree: "Речка протекала" / "Водица бѣжала"
  • the forbidden water: "Нельзя, нельзя воду пити"
  • the forbidden beating: "Нельзя, нельзя жену бити"
  • short beating, long weeping: "билъ жену часъ ... плакалъ недѣлю"

Who will comb the young man's curls Вдоль да по рѣчкѣ, вдоль по Казанкѣ8 versões, 3 livros

A young man walks along a steep riverbank with his curls, and the song asks who will get to comb them. In most versions the answer comes in steps. First an old woman, who either cannot comb or only tears or fluffs the hair. In some versions a young widow comes next; in one she weeps as she combs. Last comes a fair maiden, who knows how to comb, and in several versions she lays each hair in place. Several things change from text to text: whether the song opens with a drake swimming down the river, the colour of the curls (fair or black), and how many women take a turn. One version puts a bawdy chorus about eight girls and one lad between the verses. Another is a longer text with a hat-and-courtship scene, and in it the curls go to a girl named Katerinushka.

  • The young man on the bank: 'по бережку... по крутому / Добрый молодец идет'
  • He is with his curls: 'Сам с кудрями... разговаривает'
  • The question of who will comb them: 'Кому... кудри... достанутся расчесать?'
  • The curls end up with a girl or a named woman, after a less welcome first answer in most versions

O que esta página não pode dizer

  • Os editores escolheram o que imprimir. Muitos imprimiram uma versão e deixaram as outras em notas, que o corpus não inclui.
  • As versões foram encontradas por um modelo de IA e por palavras partilhadas. Cerca de 9 em cada 10 ligações entre livros estão certas; cerca de um terço das variantes dentro de um livro escapa.
  • A ortografia antiga, o dialeto e os erros de OCR podem parecer alterações; a comparação unifica as letras antigas e admite pequenas diferenças, mas não todas.
  • 29% das canções não têm região conhecida, e as datas de registo abrangem cerca de 150 anos: a região mistura-se com a década e o recolhedor.
  • Os números aqui descrevem estes livros. Não são testados e não mostram porque mudou uma canção.
